=== awkward-questions
blurb: The behavioural questions people dread: a layoff, a career gap, short tenures, being let go or put on a performance plan, a failed project, "why are you leaving?", "what's your biggest weakness?", having no story for a prompt, and "do you have other offers?". Honest, short, forward-looking answers, and why lying is riskier in India.
signals: You were laid off or have a gap on your resume | You've changed jobs three times in four years | You were let go or put on a PIP | You dread "what's your biggest weakness?"
prereq: star-stories

--- lesson
:::words
- **Layoff**: losing your job because the company cut roles, not because of your performance.
- **Career gap**: a period without employment.
- **PIP (performance improvement plan)**: a formal plan a company puts an employee on when it's unhappy with performance; it often ends in exit.
- **Humblebrag**: a boast disguised as a complaint or weakness ("I'm too much of a perfectionist").
- **BGV (background verification)**: checks an agency does after an offer: past employers, dates, exit records, education.
:::

## The problem in plain words

Interviewers ask these questions to check two things: **honesty** and **self-awareness**. The facts themselves (a layoff, a gap, a failure) matter much less than how you talk about them. The pattern that works for all of them is short, honest, non-bitter, and forward-looking. The pattern that fails is long, defensive, blaming or invented.

:::eli5
If a friend asks why you missed a cricket match, "I was sick, I'm fine now, I'll be at the next one" is perfect. A ten-minute story blaming the coach, the weather and your neighbour makes them suspicious even if it's all true.
:::

## Layoffs

Career sources agree: be upfront, say briefly that it was not about performance, give context (cash problems, a product shut down, a percentage of staff cut), say what you've done since, and offer references; don't ramble, which can read as overcompensating, and don't be bitter (reputable careers sources). Hiring managers generally treat layoffs as business decisions. Stigma has eased but not vanished: a Monster survey found 69% of workers feel layoff stigma hasn't improved, and one in three would hide a layoff (reputable vendor survey).

Suggested wording (not from a source):

> "My role was part of a 20% reduction when the company shut down its lending product after the funding round fell through. It wasn't performance-related, and my manager is happy to be a reference. Since then I've finished a distributed-systems course and contributed to an open-source scheduler, and I'm looking for a backend role where I can own a service end to end, which is why this team interests me."

## Career gaps

Experts quoted by Fortune (December 2024) see less stigma than before but describe hiding a gap as a dealbreaker (reputable). A 2025 study cited by Quartz found 61% of managers viewed gaps negatively, until they had context (reputable, secondary). In India, a TechGig poll found 78% of tech professionals fear a gap may cost them their dream job, with large IT firms said to take gaps "very seriously" (weak data: self-reported fear), and a Blind commenter estimates far fewer callbacks with a gap (anecdotal).

What works:
- One sentence of reason (health, family, caregiving, study, a layoff, a deliberate break, a startup attempt). You don't owe medical details.
- One or two sentences on what you did or learned.
- One sentence on why you're ready now and why this role.
- Expect to apply more widely if you have a long gap, and lean on referrals.

## Short tenures and job-hopping

Indian HR views are mixed: an Infosys HR leader quoted by People Matters says frequent changes can signal an "unstable/undependable resource unless there are very good reasons"; other HR pieces describe job-hopping as increasingly normal, with one in six tech employees changing jobs yearly (reputable, Indian HR trade press).

What works: a short, honest reason for each move that shows a pattern of growth or a clear external cause (acquisition, a team shutting down, relocation), and a statement of why you'd stay at this one. Never criticise previous employers.

## Being let go, or a PIP

Career sources agree (reputable, general careers): tell the truth; softer phrasing like "we parted ways" is acceptable; name the issue briefly; then focus on what you learned and do differently. For a PIP: you got difficult feedback, acted on it, and learned where your strengths fit best.

**Don't call a firing a layoff, especially in India.** Indian background verification firms routinely check exit records with previous employers and compare dates against provident-fund (UAN) records; a vendor study put white-collar discrepancy rates at about 6%, and offers have been revoked after discrepancy reviews (reputable press, anecdotal cases). A lie discovered at BGV can cost the offer after you've resigned.

Suggested wording (not from a source):

> "It wasn't a good fit. I moved from backend work to a role heavy on front-end performance, got direct feedback that I was slow there, worked on it with a mentor and courses, but we agreed to part ways. What I took from it is to check role scope carefully, and it's why I'm targeting backend roles where my strengths are."

## A failed project

Amazon's bar raisers want "what you've learned from a failure" and how it changed your work: "Always be honest about your own mistakes" (official, Amazon bar raiser). interviewing.io's Amazon guide says Amazon wants people "vocally self-critical, even when doing so is awkward", with real losses (lost money, a missed contract) (reputable). Structure: what you owned → what went wrong and your part in it → what you did immediately → what you changed → evidence the change stuck.

## "Why are you leaving?"

One neutral fact about your current situation, then two forward-looking reasons tied to the new role; never criticise your manager (an inference consistent with all sources). "I've been on the same platform for four years and learned a lot; I'm looking for larger-scale distributed systems and to lead design, which this role offers."

## "What's your biggest weakness?"

This is the one with peer-reviewed evidence. Sezer, Gino and Norton (2018) found that in a job-interview study, **77% of candidates humblebragged** instead of naming a real weakness, with "perfectionism" (33%) and "working too hard" (25%) the top answers, and raters were much more interested in hiring the honest answerers; Adam Grant's summary puts it at about 30% more interested (reputable, peer-reviewed).

So: a **real, non-core** weakness + a concrete fix + evidence it's improving.

> "I used to avoid conflict in design reviews: I'd raise concerns privately instead of in the meeting, which slowed decisions. Over the last year I've started writing a short 'risks' section in every design doc and raising my top concern in the review itself. Last quarter that caught a data-loss bug before launch."

Avoid weaknesses that are core to the role ("I'm not good at coding") and fake ones ("I care too much").

## No story for a prompt

interviewing.io's Amazon guide: "No answer is often better than a bad answer"; quoting an interviewer, "it's better to share no example than a bar-lowering example"; it's acceptable to say you don't have one, but dodging several questions in a row hurts (reputable). Amazon's bar raisers advise preparing "one or two work anecdotes" for each Leadership Principle (official).

Suggested wording (not from a source): "I don't have a strong example of exactly that. The closest is [X], which is about [adjacent theme]. Would that be useful, or would you prefer a different question?"

Reusing a story is fine up to about twice in a loop; if asked the same thing twice, say so and let the interviewer choose (anecdotal, Blind consensus).

## Negative questions about your manager

Amazon bar raisers warn against oversharing negative details about past managers or colleagues: it makes interviewers lose trust (official). For conflict questions, interviewing.io advises stating the conflict in the first few seconds, choosing a real sustained disagreement, not trying to prove you were right, and, if you lost, saying you voiced objections then committed (reputable).

## "Do you have other offers?"

Briefly say whether you're interviewing elsewhere; you don't need to name companies; don't bluff (reputable career sites). Pair it with genuine interest: "Yes, I'm in a couple of processes at a similar stage. This role is high on my list, so it would help to know your timeline." More in [The recruiter call](/learn/recruiter-call).

--- doubts
Q: Should I put my career gap on my resume or hide the dates?
A: Show accurate dates. In India, BGV compares your dates with provident-fund records, and a hidden gap that's discovered looks worse than an explained one. Add a one-line note if it helps ("Career break: caregiving, 2025").

Q: Isn't admitting a real weakness risky?
A: The research says the opposite: honest weaknesses get more hiring interest than humblebrags. The risk is choosing a weakness that's core to the job. Pick a real one that's improvable and show improvement.

Q: I was laid off twice in two years. Will that look bad?
A: Two layoffs during a period of industry-wide cuts is common and understood, especially with context ("both were company-wide cuts of over 15%"). Keep it brief, show what you delivered at each, and explain why this role is a stable fit.

=== language-accent
blurb: Interviewing in English when it isn't your first language: the evidence on accent bias, why thinking aloud in a weaker language is genuinely harder, how to ask for a repeat without losing face, stock phrases that free up your mind, captions, and what accommodations companies do and don't offer.
signals: You think in your first language and translate | You worry about your accent with a US or UK panel | You didn't catch the interviewer's question | You go quiet when you have to explain while thinking
prereq: stuck-blank

--- lesson
:::words
- **Non-native speaker**: someone for whom English is a second or later language.
- **Accent bias**: judging people as less competent or hireable because of how they sound.
- **Think-aloud**: explaining your reasoning out loud while you solve a problem.
- **Foreign language side effect**: a temporary drop in thinking ability while using a language you're less fluent in.
- **Captions**: live subtitles in video calls (Google Meet, Zoom).
- **Accommodation**: an adjustment a company makes to the interview for a candidate's needs (extra time, breaks, interpreters).
:::

## The problem in plain words

Coding and design interviews ask you to do two hard things at once: solve a problem and narrate your thinking. If English is your second language, the narration costs more mental effort, and accent bias in hiring is real. Neither is a reason to stay quiet; both are reasons to prepare a little differently.

:::eli5
Juggling while talking is hard. Juggling while talking in a language you learned at school is harder. The trick is to rehearse the sentences beforehand, so talking takes no effort and all your attention can go to the balls.
:::

## What the evidence says

**Accent bias is the best-documented interviewer bias.** A 2022 meta-analysis found standard-accented candidates rated as more hireable than non-standard-accented ones, more so for communication-heavy jobs, and how understandable the speaker was did *not* explain it away (strong). Huang, Frideger and Pearce (2013) found non-native accents lowered recommendations through lower perceived political skill (moderate). See [Interviewer biases against you](/learn/interviewer-biases). There is no study specific to coding interviews.

**Thinking aloud in a weaker language is genuinely harder.** Takano and Yagyu (2021) found a "foreign language side effect": Japanese students reasoned worse while speaking English at the same time than while speaking Japanese (reputable, peer-reviewed). A 2020 paper found the opposite effect in some logic tasks (a foreign language can prompt more careful thinking), so it depends on the task and proficiency. Being watched while thinking aloud already roughly halves performance for everyone (Behroozi and colleagues 2020).

For many Indian engineers interviewing with Indian panels, English is shared and accents are familiar. The effects matter most with US, UK or European interviewers, common for senior, ML and remote-first roles.

## Practical techniques

These are inferences from the research, not tested in interviews:

1. **Write before you speak.** Put the plan as comments in the editor ("// 1. sort; 2. two pointers; 3. return pairs") and then narrate from it. Text carries the load your speech would.
2. **Rehearse stock phrases** for common moves, so they cost nothing in the moment:
   - "Let me restate the problem to make sure I've understood."
   - "I'll start with a brute-force approach."
   - "This is O(n log n) because we sort once."
   - "Let me test this with an empty input."
   - "I'm going to think quietly for a moment."
3. **Announce short silent blocks.** "Let me think for twenty seconds" is fine; then summarise what you concluded.
4. **Signpost structure.** "Three steps. First… second… third…" Listeners follow structure even through an unfamiliar accent.
5. **Slow down slightly and pause.** Pausing is normal; rushing makes accents harder to follow.
6. **Use the whiteboard or editor for numbers and names.** Write "QPS = 10K", "p99 < 100 ms" as you say them.

## Asking for a repeat

Asking to repeat or rephrase is normal and cheap. Restating the question in your own words is already standard clarifying practice, so it doubles as a language check.

Suggested wording (not from a source):
- "Sorry, the audio broke up; could you say the last constraint again?"
- "Just to check I've understood: you want the shortest path, and edges can be negative?"
- "Could you give me an example input?"

## Understanding your interviewer

Interviewers' accents can be hard for you too. Google's virtual-interview guide recommends turning on Google Meet captions to follow what's said (official). Ask for captions, or for the question to be pasted into the editor: "Would you mind typing the main constraints into the doc?"

## Accommodations

Google, Amazon and Microsoft all run candidate-accommodation processes: Google's examples include extended interview time, breaks between interviews, sign-language interpreters and live captioning (official); Amazon's Applicant and Candidate Accommodation Team handles requests through your recruiter (official); Microsoft lets you request accommodations without disclosing a disability or giving medical documentation (official). These are framed around disability. **No company page found offers accommodation for limited English or lets software engineers interview in another language.**

If you have a speech, hearing or other condition, request accommodation early through the recruiter.

## Practice

- Do mock interviews in English, recorded, then listen back for one thing to fix at a time (pace, filler words, structure).
- Practise with interviewers who have accents you'll meet (US, UK); the portal's AI interviewer can help with pace and structure.
- Read problem statements aloud during solo practice.

## Interview angles

- Communication is a scored attribute everywhere, but it's scored as *clarity and structure*, not accent. Structure, signposting and written support raise the score you can control.
- Senior and behavioural rounds rely more on speech; rehearse your stories aloud until the English is automatic.

Q: Should I apologise for my English at the start?
A: No. It frames the interview around a weakness that may not matter. If something is unclear, ask for a repeat or restate; that's normal for every candidate.

=== nerves-energy
blurb: Interview anxiety measurably but modestly lowers ratings. What actually helps (practising under observation, reappraising nerves as excitement, slow breathing, protecting sleep, your usual caffeine), what doesn't, how to keep energy across four to six rounds, how to ask for breaks, and how to recover from a bad round mid-loop.
signals: Your hands shake before interviews | You sleep badly before an onsite | Round two went badly and round three is in 15 minutes | You have five virtual rounds in one day
prereq: stuck-blank

--- lesson
:::words
- **Interview anxiety**: nervousness specific to interviews. Researchers split it into five kinds: communication, appearance, social, performance, and behavioural (the body's reactions such as a racing heart).
- **Reappraisal**: relabelling arousal ("I'm excited") instead of trying to suppress it ("calm down").
- **Heart-rate variability (HRV)**: natural variation between heartbeats; higher vagal HRV is a marker of a calmer, recovered nervous system.
- **Slow-paced breathing**: breathing at about six breaths a minute (for example in for 4 seconds, out for 6).
:::

## The problem in plain words

Almost everyone is nervous before important interviews. Nerves are not the enemy; unmanaged nerves are. Research gives a small set of techniques that actually work, a few popular ones that don't, and a clear message about sleep. The rest is logistics: energy across a long day and a routine for bouncing back from a bad round.

:::eli5
A cricketer walking out to bat is nervous every time. Good ones have a routine: the same stretches, the same guard, the same deep breath. The routine doesn't remove nerves; it gives them a job.
:::

## How much anxiety matters

A meta-analysis (Powell, Stanley and Brown, 2018) found self-reported interview anxiety correlates about **−0.19** with interviewer-rated performance: a real but modest effect (reputable). In tech specifically, being watched while solving and explaining roughly **halved** performance in a small randomised study (Behroozi and colleagues 2020). The second finding matters more for you: unfamiliarity with the *audience*, not lack of skill, did the damage.

## What helps

**1. Practise the real format (strongest lever).** Mock interviews with a person, talking aloud, timed, in a shared editor. Exposure to the actual stressor is what the observed-whiteboard result points to.

**2. Reappraise nerves as excitement.** Brooks (2014): saying "I am excited" before karaoke, public speaking and maths tasks beat "calm down" on both feelings and performance; a 2025 replication found more excitement but not less anxiety (moderate, partly replicated). Anxiety and excitement are both high-arousal states, so only the label changes.

**3. Slow breathing.** A meta-analysis of breathwork trials (Fincham and colleagues, 2023; 785 adults) found small-to-medium reductions in stress and anxiety; slow breathing reliably raises vagal HRV during and right after a session (Laborde and colleagues 2022, 223 studies) (reputable; read cautiously: small evidence base and these studies measure stress, not interview performance). A simple version: in through the nose for about 4 seconds, out for about 6, for one to two minutes before a round.

**4. Protect sleep.** Short-term sleep loss reliably hurts attention and working memory (Lim and Dinges 2010, meta-analysis of 70 articles) (reputable). The last two nights before an onsite matter more than one more evening of practice. Don't cram.

**5. Your usual caffeine, not more.** The EU food-safety authority (EFSA) finds single doses up to 200 mg (about one or two cups of coffee) and daily intake up to 400 mg safe for healthy adults; higher doses (400–500 mg and up) increase anxiety, mainly in people who don't usually drink it (official). Interview day is not the day to try a double espresso.

**6. Write things down.** Under stress, working memory shrinks; writing the example, the plan and the invariant moves load onto the page. Tech Interview Handbook: "under stress… people tend to forget the most obvious things", so keep basics on a checklist (reputable).

## What doesn't (or is unproven)

- **Power posing**: failed a large replication.
- **Expressive writing** about worries before a test: failed replications (Camerer and colleagues 2018; a 173-student classroom study found no effect).
- **"Just relax"**: was the weaker condition in Brooks's experiments.
- **Food tricks**: no robust evidence either way; eat normally and keep water nearby (common sense, not sourced).

## Energy across four to six rounds

There's no data on performance decline across a long loop, so these are practical inferences:
- Eat a normal meal before; water and a light snack between rounds.
- Stand up and move between virtual rounds; look away from the screen.
- **Ask for breaks.** Google lists "breaks between interviews" among past candidate accommodations (official), and Microsoft's neurodiversity hiring builds in longer breaks (official). For virtual loops you can simply ask the recruiter for a 15–30 minute gap after the second or third round, or to split the loop across two days.

Suggested wording (not from a source): "Could we leave a 20-minute gap after the second round? I do better with a short reset."

## Recovering from a bad round mid-loop

- **You probably can't judge it.** interviewing.io found candidates' self-ratings predicted interviewer ratings poorly (R² never above about 0.26), and Gayle Laakmann McDowell notes you can't judge your performance without knowing what's normal for that question (reputable). A round that *felt* bad may not have been.
- **Each round is a fresh start.** Interviewers write feedback independently.
- **Routine**: two minutes writing down what went wrong (to get it out of your head), close the note, water, slow breaths, one line for the next round ("first five minutes: clarify, example, brute force"). Don't replay the last round.

--- doubts
Q: I'm so nervous my mind goes blank. Is something wrong with me?
A: No. Stress takes over working memory for almost everyone; the observed-whiteboard study showed big drops for ordinary students. The fix is exposure (many mocks under real conditions), routines, and writing things down. See [Going blank and getting stuck](/learn/stuck-blank).

Q: Should I take medication for interview anxiety?
A: That's a question for a doctor, not an interview guide. Some people do use prescribed medication for performance anxiety under medical advice. Don't try anything new on interview day.

Q: Is it better to do all rounds in one day or spread them out?
A: There's no data. Many people prefer one day to keep momentum; others do better split across two days with rest in between. You can ask the recruiter for either; pick based on your own energy in long mock sessions.

- **Reverse interview**: the part of the round where you ask the questions.

## The problem in plain words

Almost every round ends with "do you have any questions for me?". It's usually not scored, but it isn't zero-risk either: no questions can look like low interest, and a sharp question can end the round well. It's also your best chance to learn whether you want the job.

:::eli5
At the end of a house viewing, the owner asks "any questions?". Asking "does the roof leak in the monsoon?" shows you're serious and helps you decide. Asking "what's the address?" shows you weren't paying attention.
:::

## Are your questions scored?

No Google, Meta or Amazon source says candidate questions are a scored rubric item; the evidence points to "not scored, but can colour the written comment in a close call" (prep sites; anecdotal). Committees decide mostly from the written narrative, spending about 2–3 minutes per candidate (Hello Interview, reputable). Amazon's official advice is simply: "ask the questions that you actually want to know the answers to" (official, Amazon bar raiser). Not asking any can make you seem less interested (Tech Interview Handbook, reputable).

## How many, and when

Aim for about **three** questions, fewer if the interviewer seems rushed; avoid salary, benefits and anything already answered (reputable career sources). Keep 3–5 minutes for this part; if your problem ran over, one good question is enough.

## Questions by interviewer

From Tech Interview Handbook's lists (reputable), plus suggested additions (not from a source) where marked.

**Engineer (peer)**
- "What are the biggest engineering challenges the team is facing?"
- "What's the most costly technical decision made early on that you're living with now?"
- "What's the most technically exciting project you've worked on here?"
- "What does a typical code review look like?" *(suggested)*

**Hiring manager**
- "What would be the most important problem you'd want me to solve if I joined?"
- "What does success look like for this role in the first six months?"
- "How do you ramp up new engineers?"
- "What are the team's strengths and weaknesses, and what's being done about the weaknesses?"
- "How is on-call shared?" *(suggested)*

**Bar raiser or cross-team interviewer** (they're not on the team, so ask about culture and the bar)
- "What separates people who do well here in their first year from those who struggle?" *(suggested)*
- "Which Leadership Principle do new engineers find hardest?" *(suggested; Amazon)*
- "What's unique about working here that you haven't seen elsewhere?"

**Senior leader**
- "How does the company decide what to work on next?"
- "Which competitors worry you most, and what makes you different?"

**Recruiter (not interviewers)**: salary, leave, hybrid policy, notice-period flexibility, joining-bonus clawback terms, team location (India GCC team vs product team).

## Questions to avoid

- Anything on the careers page or already answered in the round.
- Salary, perks and leave (ask the recruiter).
- "How did I do?" (interviewers usually can't say, and it's awkward).
- Questions that sound like doubts about the work ("is there a lot of overtime?" — ask instead "what does a typical week look like, and how is on-call shared?").

## After the round

- **Thank-you notes**: a recruiter-side view is that they rarely change outcomes because feedback is usually submitted before they arrive; they don't hurt (anecdotal). A short email to the recruiter thanking the panel is enough; individual notes are optional.
- **Write down** each round's question and how it went, while it's fresh. It helps later rounds, other companies and any re-application.
- **Follow up** with the recruiter if you've heard nothing a few days after the promised date, or immediately if you have a deadline elsewhere.
- **Report** genuine problems (time lost, inappropriate questions) factually and the same day (see [Difficult interviewers](/learn/difficult-interviewers)).
- **Ask for feedback** if rejected; many companies share little, but some do.

Suggested post-loop email (not from a source):

> Hi Kavya, thank you for organising today's interviews. I enjoyed the conversations, especially the design discussion about the notification service, and I'm even more excited about the team. Please let me know if anything else would help. Best, Arun.

## Interview angles

- At team-match calls (Google, Meta), questions *are* the conversation: prepare team-specific ones (roadmap, on-call, how the team measures success).
- Senior candidates' questions should show strategic thinking: priorities, risks, how decisions are made.
