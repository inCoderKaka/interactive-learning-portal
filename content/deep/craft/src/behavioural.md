--- lesson
:::words
- **Behavioural question**: "Tell me about a time when…"; it asks for a real past situation, because past behaviour predicts future behaviour.
- **Hypothetical question**: "What would you do if…"; asks how you would act.
- **STAR**: Situation, Task, Action, Result. Microsoft's careers guidance adds **R**eflection (STAR-R); many add **L**earning (STAR-L).
- **Story bank**: a prepared set of real stories, each tagged with the themes it can answer.
- **Probe**: the interviewer's follow-up question that digs into your story.
:::

## The problem in plain words

Behavioural rounds feel like conversation, but they are scored on evidence about how you work: ownership, judgement, collaboration, dealing with failure. Amazon's official guidance says it plainly: different interviewers evaluate different Leadership Principles, each asks two or three questions about past successes or challenges "because past behavior is an indicator of future success", and you should use STAR, include metrics and cover risks, failures and growth (official). The trap is telling a long, vague story where the interviewer can't tell what *you* did.

:::eli5
When a friend asks "how did you fix your bike?", the useful answer isn't the history of the bike. It's what was broken, what you tried, what worked, and what you'd do next time.
:::

## STAR(L)

| Part | Length | Content |
|---|---|---|
| **S**ituation | 2 sentences | Context: team, product, scale, what was at stake |
| **T**ask | 1 sentence | Your responsibility or goal |
| **A**ction | ~60% of the answer | What *you* did and why: options, decisions, how you handled people |
| **R**esult | 1–2 sentences | Measurable outcome, ideally with numbers |
| **L**earning | 1 sentence | What you learned or would do differently |

Aim for **2–3 minutes** per answer, then expect two follow-ups.

## The rules that matter

- **"I", not "we", for actions.** Credit the team for results, but the interviewer needs your part: "I proposed…", "I wrote…", "I convinced…".
- **Quantify.** Latency down 40%, on-call pages from 12 to 2 a week, 3 teams unblocked, ₹2 crore saved a year. Amazon calls itself "a data-driven company".
- **Real past events**, not hypotheticals, for "tell me about a time" questions. Google's reflective questions expect real experiences, not opinions (reporting).
- **No villains.** Show empathy for the other side in conflict stories; never criticise a past manager (Amazon bar raisers warn this loses trust; official).
- **Recent and relevant.** Prefer the last two or three years and work examples over side projects (Meta guidance via aced.io).
- **Real failures.** Amazon bar raisers ask for genuine failures and what changed (official).

## The story bank

Build six to eight stories and tag each with the themes it can serve. Twelve themes cover nearly every prompt:

hardest technical problem · conflict or disagreement · failure or incident · leading without authority · ambiguity · tight deadline trade-off · mentoring someone · customer focus · going beyond scope · hard feedback received · influencing with data · changing your mind.

Amazon's bar raisers advise one or two anecdotes per Leadership Principle (official); with 16 principles, that's why tagging stories to several themes matters. Reusing a story about twice across a loop is fine (Blind consensus; anecdotal).

## Writing a story

1. Pick the situation and write **one line** of headline: "Cut checkout failures by redesigning retries during the 2025 sale."
2. Write the STAR(L) in bullet points, not paragraphs, so you don't memorise a script.
3. Find the numbers: before/after metrics, scale, money, people.
4. List the **decisions** you made and the alternatives you rejected.
5. Prepare for the probes: "what would you do differently?", "what did the other person think?", "what was the hardest part?", "how did you measure success?".
6. Say it out loud and record it; cut anything that isn't action.

## Example (shortened)

> **S**: Our payments service failed about 2% of checkouts during sale peaks, costing roughly ₹40 lakh a day. **T**: I owned reliability for the service. **A**: I traced failures to retries that double-charged or timed out. I proposed idempotency keys and a dead-letter queue, wrote the design doc, got buy-in from the two upstream teams by showing their error logs, and rolled it out behind a flag over two weeks. **R**: Failures dropped to 0.3% in the next sale; double charges stopped entirely. **L**: I now add idempotency to any payment path from day one, and I'd involve upstream teams earlier.

--- doubts
Q: Should I memorise my stories word for word?
A: No. Memorised scripts sound flat and break when probed. Memorise the headline, the key decisions and the numbers; tell the rest naturally. Practise aloud until the structure is automatic.

Q: What if my best story was a team effort?
A: Most are. Describe the team briefly, then your specific actions and decisions. If your part was small, pick a different story where you drove more.

Q: Can I use a story from college or a personal project?
A: For experienced roles, prefer work examples; Meta's guidance specifically prefers work over side projects. Use non-work stories only if they show something your work can't (leadership in a community project, for example).

## The problem in plain words

Behavioural prompts look endless, but they come from a dozen themes. If your story bank covers the themes, you can answer almost any wording. What separates strong from weak answers is predictable, and so are the follow-ups.

:::eli5
There are hundreds of exam questions about photosynthesis, but they all test the same few ideas. Learn the ideas and you can answer any wording.
:::

## The themes, what strong looks like, and the probes

| Theme | Typical prompts | Strong answers show | Expect next |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hardest problem | "Most technically challenging project?" | Concrete difficulty (scale, ambiguity, constraints), options considered, your contribution, numbers | "Why that trade-off?" "What would you change?" |
| Conflict | "Disagreed with a colleague or manager?" | Understood their view, used data or a prototype, agreed a decision process, committed, relationship kept | "What was their perspective?" "What if they weren't convinced?" |
| Failure / incident | "Time you failed or caused an incident?" | Real consequences, immediate ownership and mitigation, blameless root cause, a lasting fix | "How did you communicate it?" "What changed after?" |
| Ambiguity | "Delivered with unclear requirements?" | Found the unknowns, talked to users, wrote assumptions, small first milestone, iterated | "How did you decide what to build first?" "Which assumption was wrong?" |
| Leadership without authority | "Led something you didn't own?" | Noticed an unowned problem, built alignment (doc, demo, data), enabled others, shared credit | "How did you win over a sceptic?" "Someone not delivering?" |
| Deadline trade-off | "Quality vs speed?" | Explicit options and risks, what you cut and refused to cut, stakeholder agreement, debt paid later | "How did you make sure the debt was paid?" |
| Mentoring | "Helped someone grow?" | A specific person and gap, how you adapted, their measurable growth | "How did you give hard feedback?" |
| Hard feedback | "Critical feedback you received?" | Real feedback that stung, you sought specifics, changed behaviour, evidence | "Did you agree at the time?" |
| Customer focus | "Went out of your way for a customer?" | Understood the real need, acted, measured the effect | "How did you know it mattered?" |
| Beyond scope | "Did more than asked?" | Spotted an opportunity, judged it worth it, delivered without dropping your work | "What did you deprioritise?" |
| Changing your mind | "Changed your view based on data?" | What you believed, the evidence, how you updated publicly | "How did you tell others?" |

## Motivation questions

**"Why this company?"** Two or three specific reasons tied to the team, product or engineering challenges, plus what you'll bring. Apple treats "Why Apple?" as a real evaluation even in technical rounds; Goldman Sachs asks "why GS and not a tech company?" (research notes). Generic praise ("great culture, big brand") scores poorly.

**"Why are you leaving?"** One neutral fact, two forward-looking reasons; no criticism of your employer (see [Awkward questions](/learn/awkward-questions)).

**"Tell me about yourself."** 60–90 seconds: current role and scope, one standout achievement with numbers, why this role (see [Interviewer biases](/learn/interviewer-biases) on first impressions).

## Hypotheticals

Google uses reflective and hypothetical questions for Googleyness and leadership (reporting). For "what would you do if a teammate wasn't delivering?": gather facts first, talk privately and specifically, offer help, agree a plan, escalate transparently if needed, and, where possible, anchor in a real example: "I'd do what I did when…". A real example beats a purely theoretical answer.

## Company flavours

- **Amazon**: questions map to specific Leadership Principles; "Dive Deep", "Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit" and "Deliver Results" prompts are common; bar raisers want real failures (official).
- **Meta**: two core prompts, a proudest project and a genuine conflict (a disagreement with little tension may not count); values stressed: speed, ownership, outcomes, raising better approaches even to senior leaders (reporting).
- **Google**: reflective and hypothetical questions; real experiences, not opinions (reporting).
- **Netflix**: the culture memo is the rubric: candour, judgement, selflessness, courage (official memo; reporting).
- **Microsoft**: growth mindset: failures you learned from; deflecting failures is a red flag (official tips; reputable prep).
- **Banks**: SDLC, testing and production-support stories; "why finance?" (research notes).

Details are in [Company values side by side](/learn/values-map) and each company chapter.

The same story can score differently at different companies, because each listens for different values. Amazon wants ownership and customer obsession with data; Netflix wants candour and judgement; Microsoft wants growth from failure. You don't need different stories for each company: you need to know which part of each story to emphasise.

:::eli5
The same cricket innings can be praised for patience by one commentator and for aggression by another. Same innings; the commentator listens for different things.
:::

## Reusing stories across companies

Take one story, "Redesigned payment retries during the sale":
- At **Amazon**, lead with Customer Obsession and Dive Deep: the customer impact in rupees, how you traced the root cause in logs.
- At **Netflix**, lead with judgement and candour: the decision you made with incomplete information, and the hard conversation with the upstream team.
- At **Microsoft**, lead with growth: what went wrong in your first attempt and what you changed.
- At **Google**, lead with ambiguity and collaboration: how you scoped an unclear problem and brought teams together.

Same facts; different emphasis, different opening sentence.

A: Candour (giving and receiving hard feedback), judgement (decisions with incomplete context), selflessness and independence (freedom and responsibility). Map a real story to each and be ready to discuss the keeper test.

Q: Are values rounds really scored?
A: At several companies, yes: Airbnb's core-values interviews can veto, Atlassian assesses values with an out-of-team interviewer, and Amazon scores Leadership Principles in every round.

=== senior-behavioural
blurb: What changes in behavioural rounds at the senior (Google L5 equivalent) level: scope, influence across teams, driving decisions under ambiguity, handling conflict with leaders, raising others, and technical judgement. How to tell stories at the right scope so the round supports the level you want.
signals: You're targeting a senior level | Your stories are about features rather than projects | The interviewer asks "what was the impact on the org?" | You were downlevelled on behavioural signal
prereq: values-map

--- lesson
:::words
- **Scope**: the size of what you owned: a feature, a project, a service, several teams' work.
- **Influence**: getting other people or teams to act, without authority over them.
- **Technical judgement**: choosing well between options with real costs, and knowing what not to do.
- **Force multiplier**: someone who makes the people around them more effective (mentoring, tools, standards).
:::

## The problem in plain words

At senior level, behavioural rounds are a **levelling** tool. Aced (formerly Exponent) names system design and behavioural rounds as the main factors in levelling (reputable), and Amazon may downlevel on behavioural weakness alone (research notes). A beautifully structured story about delivering a feature well can still read as mid-level. The fix is to tell stories at senior *scope*.

:::eli5
A junior chef is judged on cooking a dish well. A head chef is judged on running the kitchen: planning the menu, training cooks, handling a supplier failing on a busy night. Same skills underneath, bigger scope.
:::

## Mid vs senior stories

| Dimension | Mid (L4) | Senior (L5) |
|---|---|---|
| Scope | Features and tasks in an existing system | A project or service end to end; often cross-team |
| Ambiguity | Clarified requirements given to you | Defined the problem and the plan when nobody had |
| Influence | Within the team | Across teams; with managers and leaders |
| Conflict | With a peer, about implementation | With other teams or leaders, about direction |
| People | Helped a teammate | Raised the team: mentoring, standards, hiring |
| Judgement | Chose a good implementation | Chose what *not* to build; managed risk |
| Results | Feature shipped | Business or system outcome, sustained |

Exponent's framing: team-level impact reads as senior; org-level impact (breadth across teams, engineers influenced, stakeholders handled) reads as staff (reputable).

## The senior story checklist

For each story, make sure you can answer:
- **What was the problem, and who decided it mattered?** Senior engineers often find the problem.
- **What options did you consider, and what did you reject?** Name the trade-offs.
- **Who did you need to convince, and how?** Docs, data, prototypes, one-to-ones.
- **What did you delegate or enable others to do?**
- **What went wrong, and how did you handle it?**
- **What was the measured outcome, and did it last?**

## Common senior prompts

- "Tell me about a technical decision you drove that others disagreed with."
- "Describe a time you had to influence another team's roadmap."
- "Tell me about a project you led from an unclear idea to production."
- "How have you made your team better?"
- "Tell me about a time you decided *not* to do something."
- "Describe a time you disagreed with a leader's direction."

## Conflict with leaders

Senior conflict stories involve direction, not just implementation: a deadline, an architecture, a priority. Show: you raised it early, with data; you proposed options; you respected the decision process; you committed once decided ("disagree and commit", an Amazon principle); and you kept the relationship. Raising better approaches even to senior leaders is explicitly valued at Meta (reporting).

## Raising others

Senior engineers are force multipliers. Stories: mentoring someone to promotion, introducing design reviews or testing standards adopted by other teams, improving on-call, leading hiring loops, writing onboarding docs. Measure it: "two engineers I mentored now own services", "incidents fell 40% after the review process".

## Preparing

1. Rewrite your story bank's headlines at senior scope. "Built the retry library" becomes "Led the reliability overhaul across three payment services".
2. For each, prepare the decision points and the people part.
3. Practise with a mock interviewer who pushes on scope: "what was *your* role?", "what did the org gain?".
4. Align with the [level](/learn/levels-decided) you agreed with the recruiter.

## India note

Engineers moving from IT services or banks to product companies often have strong delivery stories but less ownership of product direction. Pick the stories where you defined the problem or influenced beyond your team, even if the project was smaller.

--- doubts
Q: I've never managed anyone. Can I show senior leadership?
A: Yes. Senior software engineers lead without managing: they drive designs, influence other teams, mentor, and set standards. Those are exactly the stories senior rounds look for.

Q: My biggest projects were at a services company for a client. Do they count?
A: Yes, if you describe your decisions and influence: scoping with the client, choosing the architecture, leading the delivery team. Avoid stories where you only implemented a given design.

Q: What if the interviewer says "that sounds like a team effort"?
A: Agree, then be specific: "It was; my part was proposing the approach, writing the design doc, convincing the data team and leading the rollout." Prepare these sentences in advance.
