--- lesson
:::words
- **Pretraining corpus**: the trillions of tokens of text (and code) a base model learns from.
- **Common Crawl**: a public, monthly snapshot of billions of web pages; the raw material of most corpora.
- **Quality filter**: a rule or classifier that keeps well-written, informative text and drops spam and boilerplate.
- **Deduplication**: removing exact and near-duplicate documents.
- **Data mixture**: the proportions of each source (web, code, books, maths, other languages) in training.
- **Contamination**: test-set questions leaking into training data, inflating benchmark scores.
- **PII**: personally identifiable information, such as emails and phone numbers.
:::

## The problem in plain words

A language model is, to a first approximation, a compression of its training data. What's in the data (and how clean it is) determines what the model knows, how it writes, which languages it handles, and which mistakes it repeats. Modern LLMs train on 10–40 trillion tokens; most of the engineering in a pretraining effort goes into building that corpus. Data-quality questions are common for research engineer and MLE roles on LLM teams.

:::eli5
Teaching someone by having them read the whole internet: you'd first throw away the spam, the endless copies of the same page, the gibberish and the private phone numbers, and make sure they read enough textbooks and good code, not just comment sections. That curation is most of the work.
:::

## Sources

- **Web**: Common Crawl, filtered (C4, RefinedWeb, FineWeb, DCLM): the bulk.
- **Code**: GitHub-style repositories (The Stack), with licence filtering and deduplication; code improves reasoning even for non-code tasks.
- **Books, papers, encyclopedias**: high quality, smaller volume (arXiv, Wikipedia, open books).
- **Maths and science**: filtered web pages with equations, textbooks, problem sets.
- **Multilingual** web and parallel text.
- **Dialogue, Q&A forums**: conversational style.
- **Synthetic data**: generated by existing models (textbook-style explanations, rewritten web text, maths solutions verified by code). Increasingly important; risks include homogeneity and errors.

## The cleaning pipeline

1. **Extraction**: HTML to text, removing menus, ads, cookie banners (tools like trafilatura); keep structure (headings, code blocks, tables, LaTeX).
2. **Language identification** (fastText classifiers), routing per language.
3. **Heuristic filters**: document length, ratio of symbols to words, mean word length, fraction of lines ending in punctuation, repeated lines and n-grams, "lorem ipsum", bad-word lists (carefully: they remove useful content too).
4. **Model-based quality filters**: a classifier trained to distinguish reference-quality text (Wikipedia, educational pages, or pages an LLM rated as educational) from random web text; keep the top fraction. FineWeb-Edu showed large benchmark gains from educational-quality filtering.
5. **Deduplication**:
   - Exact: hashes of documents, lines or paragraphs.
   - Near-duplicate: **MinHash** signatures of shingles (word n-grams) with **locality-sensitive hashing** to find pairs with high Jaccard similarity; drop all but one.
   - Why it matters: duplicates waste compute, cause memorisation (privacy risk), and hurt quality; deduplicated data trains better models for the same tokens.
6. **Safety and privacy**: remove or mask PII (emails, phone numbers, ID numbers), filter toxic or illegal content, respect robots.txt and opt-outs, licensing.
7. **Decontamination**: remove documents overlapping with evaluation benchmarks (n-gram overlap with test sets), so scores reflect generalisation.

## Mixtures and curriculum

The **mixture** (e.g. ~60% web, ~20% code, ~10% maths/science, ~10% books/papers/multilingual) strongly affects capabilities. Teams tune it with small-scale experiments (train small models on candidate mixtures, measure downstream evaluation; methods like DoReMi learn weights). Common practices:

- **Upsample** high-quality sources (several epochs of Wikipedia/books) while seeing most web text once.
- **Annealing / mid-training**: at the end of pretraining, lower the learning rate and shift the mixture toward the highest-quality and specialised data (maths, code, long documents, instruction-like text); it yields outsized gains.
- **Multi-epoch limits**: repeating data more than ~4 times gives diminishing returns.

## Data at scale

Trillions of tokens means petabytes of raw text: pipelines run on distributed systems (Spark, Beam/Dataflow, Ray), stored as sharded, tokenized binary files streamed to trainers in a deterministic shuffled order so training can resume exactly.

## Interview angles

- "Build a pretraining corpus." Sources and licences → extraction → language ID → heuristics → quality classifier → exact + MinHash dedup → PII/toxicity → decontamination → mixture tuned by small-scale ablations → tokenization and sharding.
- "Why dedupe?" Compute waste, memorisation and privacy leakage, and better quality per token.
- "Benchmark scores look too good." Contamination: n-gram overlap checks, held-out private evals, rephrased test sets.

--- doubts
Q: Is more data always better than cleaner data?
A: At a fixed compute budget, cleaner data usually wins: deduplicated, quality-filtered corpora train better models per token than raw ones. But filtering too aggressively removes diversity (dialects, informal writing, niche topics) and can bias the model; teams balance quality with coverage.

Q: Doesn't training on synthetic data make models worse ("model collapse")?
A: Repeatedly training models only on their own outputs degrades diversity and accuracy. Used carefully (mixed with real data, verified, diverse prompts, focused on areas like maths where answers can be checked), synthetic data improves models. Many recent models rely heavily on it.

Q: Why is benchmark contamination so hard to rule out?
A: Test questions spread across the web (forums, study sites, translations, paraphrases), and n-gram overlap misses paraphrases. Teams use multiple detection methods, private or freshly written test sets, and look for suspicious gaps between performance on original versus rephrased questions.

--- exercises
[estimate] A raw crawl has 250 trillion tokens. Quality filtering keeps 20%, deduplication removes 60% of what remains, and safety filters drop another 5%. How many tokens are left?
A: $250 \times 0.2 = 50$T; $\times 0.4 = 20$T; $\times 0.95 = 19$T tokens: enough for one large training run, which is why web quality and dedup thresholds are tuned so carefully.

[design] Implement near-duplicate detection for 1 billion web documents. Outline the approach.
A: For each document, build shingles (5-word n-grams), compute a MinHash signature of ~128 hashes (estimating Jaccard similarity), split into bands (e.g. 16 bands × 8 rows) and hash each band to buckets (LSH): documents sharing any bucket become candidate pairs; verify candidates by estimated Jaccard > 0.8; build connected components and keep one document per component (prefer the highest-quality). Run as a distributed job (Spark/Beam), with signatures computed in a map stage and grouping by band hash.

[break] A team's new model scores 92% on a coding benchmark, but users find it much weaker than a model scoring 80%. Name likely causes.
A: Contamination (benchmark problems and solutions present in training data), overfitting to the benchmark's format, or evaluation differences (prompt format, sampling). Check n-gram overlap between training data and the benchmark, evaluate on freshly written problems, and test on realistic user tasks.

- Over-aggressive word-list filters that remove whole dialects or topics
- Ignoring benchmark contamination
- Leaving PII in the corpus
- Choosing mixtures without small-scale ablations

=== scaling-laws
blurb: How loss falls with parameters, data and compute: the Kaplan power laws, compute C ≈ 6ND, Chinchilla's compute-optimal ratio of about 20 tokens per parameter, why modern models over-train for cheaper inference, emergent abilities debated, and using small runs to predict big ones.
signals: "Given a compute budget, how big should the model be?" | "Explain Chinchilla" | "Why train an 8B model on 15T tokens?" | Planning experiments and forecasting loss
widgets: scaling
prereq: transformer-block, optimisers

--- lesson
:::words
- **Scaling law**: a formula predicting a model's loss from its size, data and compute, usually a power law.
- **Power law**: $y = a x^{-b}$; a straight line on a log–log plot.
- **N**: number of parameters. **D**: number of training tokens. **C**: training compute in FLOPs.
- **Compute-optimal**: the split between model size and data that gives the lowest loss for a fixed compute budget.
- **Over-training**: training a model on far more tokens than compute-optimal, to get a smaller model that is cheaper to serve.
- **Emergent ability**: a capability that seems to appear suddenly at some scale.
:::

## The problem in plain words

Training a frontier model costs tens to hundreds of millions of dollars, so you can't experiment at full size. **Scaling laws** let you train many small models, fit a curve, and predict how a big one will do, and decide how to spend a compute budget: a bigger model on less data, or a smaller model on more? The answers shaped every major LLM since 2020, and the reasoning (with the $6ND$ rule) comes up in interviews.

:::eli5
Baking: more flour (data) and a bigger oven (parameters) both help, but with a fixed amount of gas (compute), there's a best combination. A huge oven with a spoonful of flour wastes heat; a tiny oven with a mountain of flour can't bake it all. Scaling laws are the recipe for the best mix.
:::

## Compute: C ≈ 6ND

Each training token needs about 2 FLOPs per parameter forward and 4 backward:

$$C \approx 6ND$$

A 7B model on 2T tokens: $6 \times 7\times10^9 \times 2\times10^{12} = 8.4\times10^{22}$ FLOPs. At 400 TFLOP/s effective per GPU (40% MFU on ~1,000 TFLOP/s chips), that's $2.1\times10^8$ GPU-seconds ≈ 58,000 GPU-hours.

## The power laws

Kaplan et al. (OpenAI, 2020) found test loss falls as a power law in each resource when the others aren't limiting:

$$L(N) \approx \left(\frac{N_c}{N}\right)^{\alpha_N}, \quad L(D) \approx \left(\frac{D_c}{D}\right)^{\alpha_D}, \quad L(C) \approx \left(\frac{C_c}{C}\right)^{\alpha_C}$$

with small exponents (~0.05–0.1): every 10× more compute gives a steady, predictable drop. Architecture details (depth vs width) matter much less than scale. Kaplan concluded models should grow faster than data.

## Chinchilla: compute-optimal training

Hoffmann et al. (DeepMind, 2022) fit a joint law

$$L(N, D) = E + \frac{A}{N^\alpha} + \frac{B}{D^\beta}$$

($E$ is the irreducible loss of language) and found that for compute-optimal training **N and D should scale equally**: about **20 tokens per parameter**. Their 70B Chinchilla, trained on 1.4T tokens, beat the 280B Gopher trained on 300B tokens with the same compute. Earlier giant models (GPT-3: 175B on 300B tokens, ~1.7 tokens/parameter) were badly under-trained. Explore the trade-off below: fix a compute budget, vary the model size, and watch predicted loss find its minimum:

[[widget:scaling]]

Given $C$, compute-optimal $N \approx \sqrt{C/120}$ and $D \approx 20N$ (from $C = 6N \cdot 20N$). For $C = 10^{24}$: $N \approx 91$B, $D \approx 1.8$T.

## Over-training for inference

Chinchilla minimises **training** cost for a given loss. But a model is trained once and served billions of times; serving cost scales with $N$. So it's often better to train a **smaller model on far more tokens** than compute-optimal: Llama 3 8B saw ~15T tokens (~1,900 tokens/parameter) and kept improving. Loss keeps falling with more data, just less efficiently per training FLOP. Total-cost-optimal training (training + lifetime inference) favours over-training whenever usage is high.

## Using scaling laws in practice

- Train a ladder of small models (e.g. 10M–1B) on the same data and recipe; fit the law; extrapolate the target run's loss before committing. GPT-4's technical report noted its final loss was predicted from runs with 1/10,000 the compute.
- Compare architectures, data mixtures or tokenizers by their scaling curves, not one point.
- Hyperparameters (learning rate, batch size) also follow trends with scale; μP (maximal update parametrisation) lets hyperparameters tuned on small models transfer to large ones.
- Downstream task accuracy is less predictable than loss; it often follows a sigmoid in log-compute.

## Emergence

Some abilities (multi-step arithmetic, certain reasoning tasks) appear to jump from near-random to good at a scale threshold. Later work (Schaeffer et al., 2023) showed many "emergent" jumps come from **all-or-nothing metrics** like exact match: with smoother metrics (token-level accuracy, log-likelihood), progress is gradual. Some genuinely threshold-like behaviour may remain (in-context learning of complex tasks), but "loss improves smoothly; some metrics jump" is the safe statement.

## Beyond pretraining scale

Recent gains also come from **post-training** (alignment) and **test-time compute** (reasoning models that think longer: reasoning chapter), which have their own scaling trends, and from data quality, which shifts the curves downward.

## Interview angles

- "Budget of 10²⁴ FLOPs: size?" ~90B params on ~1.8T tokens if training-optimal; smaller and longer if serving cost dominates.
- "Explain C ≈ 6ND." 2 FLOPs/param/token forward, 4 backward.
- "Why was GPT-3 under-trained?" ~1.7 tokens per parameter versus Chinchilla's ~20.
- "Are emergent abilities real?" Smooth loss, threshold-looking metrics; depends on the metric.

--- doubts
Q: If loss keeps improving with scale, why not just build ever bigger models?
A: Each 10× in compute buys a smaller absolute improvement (power law with a small exponent), and compute, data, energy and money are finite. High-quality data may run out before compute does. And serving costs grow with size, which is why smaller over-trained models and test-time compute have become popular.

Q: Does the 20 tokens per parameter rule always hold?
A: It's an empirical fit for one family of models and data. Different data quality, architectures (MoE), or objectives shift the ratio; later analyses found somewhat different constants. Treat it as an order of magnitude, and remember it optimises training compute only.

Q: Why is downstream accuracy harder to predict than loss?
A: Tasks depend on specific capabilities that may only become reliable after loss crosses some level, and accuracy metrics are discrete. Loss is a smooth average over all tokens. Teams predict loss first, then map loss to task performance using earlier models.

--- exercises
[estimate] You have 2,048 GPUs at 1,000 TFLOP/s peak (bf16) for 60 days at 40% MFU. What's your compute budget, and the Chinchilla-optimal model size and tokens?
A: $2048 \times 10^{15} \times 0.4 \times 60 \times 86400 \approx 4.2\times10^{24}$ FLOPs. $N \approx \sqrt{4.2\times10^{24}/120} \approx 1.9\times10^{11}$ (~190B), $D \approx 3.8$T tokens. If the model will be served heavily, consider ~70B on ~10T tokens instead.

[design] You must choose between two data mixtures for a planned 70B run. How do you decide without training 70B twice?
A: Train a ladder of models (e.g. 150M, 400M, 1B, 3B) on each mixture with identical recipes and token budgets scaled per size; fit scaling curves of validation loss (on a neutral held-out set) and of key downstream evaluations; compare the curves' offsets and slopes and extrapolate to 70B. Check that the ranking is stable across sizes; if curves cross, prefer the one better at the larger sizes.

[break] A team reports their 7B model "suddenly gained" 3-digit multiplication between two checkpoints and claims emergence. What would you check?
A: Whether the metric is exact match (all digits correct) while per-digit accuracy improved gradually; plot log-likelihood of the correct answer and per-digit accuracy across checkpoints. Also check data changes (annealing on maths data near those checkpoints) and evaluation noise.

=== distributed-training
blurb: Training models that don't fit on one GPU: data parallelism and all-reduce, ZeRO stages and FSDP, tensor (intra-layer) parallelism, pipeline parallelism and its bubble, sequence and context parallelism, 3-D/4-D parallelism layouts, communication costs, and fault tolerance at thousands of GPUs.

--- lesson
:::words
- **Data parallelism (DP)**: every GPU holds the whole model and processes different examples; gradients are averaged.
- **All-reduce**: a collective operation that sums (or averages) a tensor across all GPUs and gives everyone the result.
- **ZeRO / FSDP**: data parallelism that shards (splits) optimiser state, gradients and even parameters across GPUs instead of replicating them.
- **Tensor parallelism (TP)**: splitting individual weight matrices across GPUs, so each computes part of every layer.
- **Pipeline parallelism (PP)**: putting different layers on different GPUs and passing activations along like an assembly line.
- **Micro-batch**: a slice of the batch that flows through the pipeline.
- **Bubble**: idle time in a pipeline while it fills and drains.
- **Interconnect**: the links between GPUs (NVLink within a server, InfiniBand or Ethernet between servers).
:::

## The problem in plain words

A 70B model needs ~140 GB just for its weights in bf16, and over 1 TB once you add gradients, optimiser state and activations for training (training-memory page). No single GPU holds that, and even if it did, training on trillions of tokens would take decades. So training is split across hundreds to tens of thousands of chips. The art is splitting it so the GPUs spend their time computing rather than waiting for each other. Interviewers for LLM infrastructure and research-engineering roles probe this directly.

:::eli5
Writing an encyclopaedia with a thousand authors. You can give each author different chapters to draft and then agree on edits (data parallel), split each page into columns written by different people at once (tensor parallel), or form an assembly line where one person writes, the next edits, the next typesets (pipeline). Big projects combine all three and spend a lot of effort on the meetings in between.
:::

## Data parallelism

Each of $G$ GPUs holds a full copy, processes its own micro-batch, computes gradients, then an **all-reduce** averages the gradients so every replica takes the same step. Ring all-reduce moves about $2(G-1)/G \approx 2\times$ the gradient size per GPU regardless of $G$, so DP scales well, overlapping communication with the backward pass (bucketed all-reduce, as in PyTorch DDP). Limit: every GPU must fit the whole model plus optimiser state.

## ZeRO and FSDP: shard the redundancy

Plain DP stores $G$ identical copies of everything. ZeRO (DeepSpeed) removes the duplication in stages:

- **Stage 1**: shard optimiser state (Adam's two moments + fp32 master weights: 12 bytes/param) across GPUs.
- **Stage 2**: also shard gradients (reduce-scatter instead of all-reduce).
- **Stage 3**: also shard parameters; each layer's weights are **all-gathered** just before use and freed after.

Memory per GPU drops roughly by $G$ for the sharded parts, at the cost of more communication (stage 3 ≈ 1.5× DP's). PyTorch **FSDP** implements stage-3-style sharding (FSDP2 per-parameter sharding). It's the default way to train models up to tens of billions of parameters on many GPUs without model-parallel code changes.

## Tensor parallelism

Split each layer's matrices across GPUs (Megatron-LM style). For an MLP $Y = \text{GeLU}(XA)B$: split $A$ by **columns** (each GPU computes part of the hidden layer independently), then $B$ by **rows**; one all-reduce combines the partial outputs. Attention splits naturally by heads. Two all-reduces per transformer layer in forward (and two in backward) on activations: very communication-heavy, so TP is kept **inside a server** with fast NVLink (TP size 2–8).

## Pipeline parallelism

Assign consecutive layers to stages on different GPUs; activations flow forward stage to stage, gradients flow back. Naively only one stage works at a time. Splitting the batch into $m$ **micro-batches** keeps stages busy; the idle **bubble** fraction for $p$ stages is about

$$\frac{p - 1}{m + p - 1}$$

so you want $m \gg p$. Schedules: GPipe (all forwards then all backwards), **1F1B** (interleave one forward, one backward to cap activation memory), interleaved virtual stages (smaller bubble, more communication), zero-bubble schedules. PP communicates only activations at stage boundaries (point-to-point), so it works across servers.

## Sequence and context parallelism

Very long sequences make activations (and attention) too big for one GPU. **Sequence parallelism** shards the norm/dropout activations along the sequence alongside TP; **context parallelism** / **ring attention** splits a long sequence across GPUs, passing key/value blocks around a ring so each GPU computes attention for its chunk of queries. Essential for 128k+ context training.

## Putting it together (3-D / 4-D parallelism)

A typical large run: TP within each 8-GPU server, PP across a few servers, (context parallelism for long sequences), and DP/FSDP across the rest. Example for a 70B model on 1,024 GPUs: TP = 8, PP = 4, DP = 32 (each DP replica is 32 GPUs). Choose by:

- Fit memory first (TP/PP/FSDP), then use DP for throughput.
- Keep the heaviest communication (TP) on the fastest links.
- Watch **global batch size**: DP size × micro-batch × accumulation must stay near the critical batch size.
- MoE adds **expert parallelism** with all-to-all (mixture-of-experts page).

## Running for months on thousands of GPUs

- **Failures are routine**: at 10,000+ GPUs, some hardware fails every few hours. Frequent asynchronous checkpointing, automatic restart, hot spares, and health checks for "silent" slow or faulty nodes.
- **Loss spikes**: roll back to a checkpoint and skip the offending data, lower the learning rate.
- **Stragglers**: one slow GPU stalls synchronous training; detect and replace.
- **Determinism and reproducibility** of data order for exact resumption.
- Google trains on TPU pods with JAX/XLA (pjit/GSPMD sharding annotations express all these parallelisms declaratively).

--- notes
## DP
Replicas + all-reduce grads (ring ≈ 2× grad bytes/GPU). Overlap with backward (DDP buckets). Needs full model per GPU.
## ZeRO / FSDP
S1 optimiser state (12 B/param) · S2 + grads (reduce-scatter) · S3 + params (all-gather per layer). ~÷G memory; S3 ≈ 1.5× DP comm.
FSDP (PyTorch) = ZeRO-3 style; default up to tens of B params.
## TP
Megatron: MLP A by columns, B by rows → 1 all-reduce; attention by heads. 2 all-reduces/layer fwd (+2 bwd). NVLink, size 2–8.
## PP
Layers per stage, point-to-point activations. Bubble (p−1)/(m+p−1) → m ≫ p. GPipe, 1F1B, interleaved, zero-bubble.
## SP / CP
Sequence parallel (norm/dropout acts with TP). Context/ring attention for 128k+.
## Layout
TP in-node, PP across nodes, DP/FSDP outer; (+CP, +EP for MoE). 70B on 1024: TP8 × PP4 × DP32.
Fit memory first, then DP for throughput. Global batch near critical.
## Ops
Failures every few hours at 10k GPUs: async checkpoints, restart, spares, health checks, straggler detection. Loss-spike rollback + skip data. Deterministic data order. TPU: JAX pjit/GSPMD.

--- doubts
Q: Why not use only data parallelism with FSDP for everything?
A: FSDP all-gathers each layer's full weights on every GPU before computing it; for very large layers and many GPUs, the all-gather traffic and the activation memory (still full per GPU) become limiting. Tensor and pipeline parallelism split the computation itself, reducing per-GPU activations and allowing models whose single layers are huge.

Q: Does pipeline parallelism change the maths of training?
A: With synchronous schedules (GPipe, 1F1B), no: gradients are accumulated over all micro-batches before one optimiser step, so the result equals training on the whole batch. Asynchronous pipelines (stale weights) change the maths and are rarely used for LLMs.

Q: How long does a checkpoint of a 70B model take to save?
A: Model + optimiser state is ~1 TB (16 bytes/param). Written in parallel by all GPUs to a fast distributed file system, it can take tens of seconds to minutes; asynchronous checkpointing copies to CPU memory quickly and writes in the background so training continues.

--- exercises
[estimate] A pipeline has 8 stages. How many micro-batches keep the bubble under 10%?
A: $(p - 1)/(m + p - 1) < 0.1 \Rightarrow 7/(m + 7) < 0.1 \Rightarrow m > 63$. Use at least 64 micro-batches per step (or interleaved schedules to reduce the bubble with fewer).

[design] Plan parallelism for a 175B dense model on 2,048 GPUs (8 per server, NVLink inside, InfiniBand between) with sequence length 8k.
A: Memory: weights 350 GB bf16; with fp32 optimiser state ~2.8 TB total. TP = 8 within each server (splits weights and activations 8×). PP = 8 across servers: each stage holds 1/64 of the model (~44 GB of training state per GPU with optimiser sharding across DP). DP = 2048 / 64 = 32, using ZeRO-1 for optimiser state. Micro-batches ≥ 64 per step for a small bubble; global batch ≈ 32 replicas × 64 micro-batches × 1 sequence × 8k tokens ≈ 16M tokens. Activation checkpointing as needed. [choose] A 13B model fits on 8 GPUs with FSDP but runs at 25% MFU. Communication profiling shows all-gathers dominate. Options?
A: Increase per-GPU micro-batch (more compute per all-gather), overlap/prefetch all-gathers, use hybrid sharding (shard within a node, replicate across nodes so the expensive all-gathers stay on NVLink), or switch to TP = 2 within the node to reduce the size of gathered parameters. Also check activation checkpointing isn't recomputing more than needed.

- Too few micro-batches for the number of pipeline stages

- Ignoring activation memory when planning sharding

--- lesson
:::words
- **Weights**: the model's parameters.
- **Gradients**: one number per parameter, computed in the backward pass.
- **Optimiser state**: extra numbers per parameter the optimiser keeps (Adam: two moving averages).
- **Master weights**: a full-precision copy of the weights kept for accurate updates in mixed-precision training.
- **Activations**: intermediate values from the forward pass, saved for the backward pass.
- **Activation checkpointing**: saving fewer activations and recomputing them during the backward pass.
:::

## The problem in plain words

Running a 7B model for inference needs about 14 GB. Training the same model with Adam needs well over 100 GB before counting activations. Knowing where each byte goes lets you answer the very common question "what hardware do I need to train or fine-tune this?", decide between full fine-tuning and parameter-efficient methods, and debug out-of-memory errors.

:::eli5
Inference is reading a book. Training is reading it while keeping notes on every page about how to improve it (gradients), two running summaries of all past notes (Adam's state), a careful master copy of the book in full detail, and scratch paper of every intermediate thought (activations). The notes take far more space than the book.
:::

## The per-parameter budget (mixed precision with Adam)

| Item | Bytes per parameter |
|---|---|
| bf16 weights (used in forward/backward) | 2 |
| bf16 (or fp32) gradients | 2 (or 4) |
| fp32 master weights | 4 |
| Adam first moment $m$ (fp32) | 4 |
| Adam second moment $v$ (fp32) | 4 |
| **Total** | **≈ 16** |

So **training state ≈ 16 bytes × parameters**: 7B → ~112 GB; 70B → ~1.1 TB. Inference needs only the 2 bytes of weights (plus the KV cache). That's why full fine-tuning needs multiple GPUs or sharding (ZeRO/FSDP divides this across GPUs: distributed-training page).

## Activations

Saved forward values for backward scale with **batch × sequence length × width × layers**. A rough formula for a transformer layer (Korthikanti et al.): about $sbh(34 + 5as/h)$ bytes in 16-bit without recomputation, where $s$ = sequence length, $b$ = micro-batch, $h$ = hidden size, $a$ = heads. The second term is the attention score matrices, which FlashAttention avoids storing, leaving roughly $34\,sbh$ bytes per layer.

Example: $h = 4096$, $s = 4096$, $b = 1$, 32 layers: $34 \times 4096 \times 4096 \times 32 \approx 18$ GB per sequence. Activations grow linearly with batch and sequence length, so they often decide the micro-batch size.

## Reducing memory

- **Activation checkpointing**: save only each layer's input; recompute the layer in backward. Activation memory drops to about $2sbh$ per layer (plus one layer's full activations); costs ~33% more compute (one extra forward). Selective checkpointing recomputes only cheap-to-recompute parts (like attention scores).
- **Micro-batches + gradient accumulation**: smaller activations per step, same effective batch.
- **Sharding** (ZeRO/FSDP): split weights, gradients and optimiser state across GPUs.
- **Lower-precision optimiser state**: 8-bit Adam (block-wise quantised moments) saves ~6 bytes/param; Adafactor factorises the second moment (row and column statistics) to near zero; Lion keeps one moment.
- **CPU/NVMe offload** (ZeRO-Offload/Infinity): move optimiser state off the GPU; slower.
- **Parameter-efficient fine-tuning**: LoRA trains tiny adapters, so gradients and optimiser state exist only for them; QLoRA also keeps the frozen base in 4-bit: a 7B model fine-tunes in ~6–10 GB (lora-qlora page).
- **FP8 training** on newest hardware reduces activation and weight memory further.

## Worked examples

- **Inference, 7B, bf16**: 14 GB weights + KV cache (e.g. 2–8 GB): one 24 GB GPU.
- **Full fine-tune, 7B**: 112 GB state + activations: e.g. 4× 80 GB GPUs with FSDP and checkpointing.
- **LoRA, 7B, bf16 base**: 14 GB frozen weights + small adapter state + activations: one 40–80 GB GPU.
- **QLoRA, 7B**: ~4 GB base in 4-bit + adapters + activations: one 16–24 GB GPU.
- **Pretraining 70B**: ~1.1 TB state + activations: dozens of GPUs minimum, hundreds to thousands for speed.

## Interview angles

- "Memory to fine-tune a 7B model?" 16 bytes/param for state (~112 GB) + activations; or LoRA/QLoRA to fit on one GPU.
- "Out of memory at step one." Reduce micro-batch/sequence length, enable activation checkpointing, shard (FSDP), lower-precision optimiser, LoRA.
- "Why does training need ~8× inference memory?" Gradients, master weights and two Adam moments on top of bf16 weights, plus activations.

--- doubts
Q: Why keep fp32 master weights if the model computes in bf16?
A: Updates are often tiny relative to the weights (learning rate × gradient ≈ 1e-5 of the value). In bf16, with only ~3 significant decimal digits, adding such a small update would often round to no change at all. Accumulating updates into fp32 master weights keeps them; the bf16 copy is refreshed from the master each step.

Q: Do gradients need to be stored for all parameters at once?
A: In standard training, yes until the optimiser step. With gradient sharding (ZeRO-2/3) each GPU keeps only its shard; some fused optimisers apply updates layer by layer during backward and free each gradient immediately.

Q: Does activation checkpointing change the results?
A: No: recomputed activations are identical (barring non-deterministic kernels or dropout, which reuses the saved random state). It only trades compute for memory.
