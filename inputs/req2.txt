Some honest corrections to what the video implies:
- It isn't free. Agora bills per agent minute after its free allowance, on whoever's Agora project runs it. If public users can start voice sessions, you pay for their minutes.
- The app gains server code. Agora needs a server route that holds the App Certificate and issues short-lived tokens (a Vercel function). Today Ascent is entirely in the browser with keys kept there.
- "It interrupts you" mostly means fast turn-taking. It replies after about 0.5 s of silence, and you can talk over it. For the interviewer to cut in on its own ("you've been silent for 40 s", "that database won't hold at this write rate"), our server has to watch whiteboard and code changes and trigger it. Customizations

Setup screen
- Choose a pool of topics or questions, and the question is drawn at random from that pool. The current /mock page deliberately won't let you pick, as a bias guard, and choosing a pool keeps that intent.
- Tone: friendly guide, neutral Google, grilling, or a quiet note-taker.
- Level (L4 or L5 bar), length, interviewer voice, and pressure. Pressure sets how much silence the interviewer allows before speaking.

Google realism
- The full arc: "Tell me about yourself" (about 5 min), then the main problem, then "Anything you'd like to ask me?" with the interviewer answering as a fictional Google engineer.
- A plain-text code doc with no compiler and optional no highlighting, which already exists.
- An Excalidraw whiteboard (MIT licensed) for HLD and LLD rounds.
- "Can I take a minute to think?" pauses the interviewer.
- Hints are available but recorded as costing signal.

Whiteboard into context, cheaply
- Turn the Excalidraw drawing into text such as Client → LB → API ×3 → Redis; API → Postgres (label: "sharded by user_id").
- Send only what changed every few seconds, so no vision model is needed.
- A "look at my diagram" button sends a picture of the board to a vision model only when you ask.

Personal, driven by what you've learned
- The roadmap suggests mocks from completed topics plus weak spots: spaced-review lapses, poor confidence calibration, low-scoring design stages.
- A "readiness mock" mixes two learned topics with one unseen.
- Mock results feed back into spaced review and the roadmap.
- An optional coach mode lets the interviewer remember last time ("you skipped capacity estimates again"). Real mode keeps it blind, as a real interviewer would be.

Curveballs
- A requirement changes mid-interview.
- The interviewer suggests a bad idea to see if you push back.
- An out-of-curriculum design.
- "Find my ceiling" probes beyond L5.
- A production-incident scenario.
- "Explain it to a PM."
- A chat about recent AI news. This needs a fresh news feed or a model that can search the web at the start of the session; otherwise the model's knowledge is stale.

Ideas beyond your list
- Replay: scrub through audio, transcript and whiteboard together. Show filler words, how much you talked versus the interviewer, the longest silence, and time until your first clarifying question.
- Hiring-committee packet: each rating comes with timestamped quotes as evidence.
- Loop day: 4–5 back-to-back rounds with different interviewers, breaks between, and a final committee decision.
- Reverse mode: you interview an AI candidate who makes planted mistakes, and you have to catch them. It teaches the rubric fast.
- Peer mode: Agora natively supports multiple people in one call, so two users interview each other while the AI observes and scores. This is the cheapest realistic practice.
- Cost guard: a monthly minute budget, a cheap model during the round with a strong model only for the scorecard, and an automatic switch to free browser voice when the budget runs out.
- Privacy mode: browser voice plus your local-server LLM, so nothing leaves your machine.

Suggested order

1. Phase 1 (no Agora, free, fits today's app): the setup screen (pool, tone, intro and questions arc, curveballs), Excalidraw with the drawing-to-text step, browser voice in the existing mock, and roadmap-suggested mocks.
2. Phase 2: the Agora engine through our own /api/interviewer endpoint (option B), plus the interviewer cutting in when the whiteboard or code changes.
3. Phase 3: replay and analytics, loop day, peer mode, reverse mode, the AI-news round.

Skipping Agora

For one learner talking to an AI, Agora adds two things: a managed network for carrying audio, and a ready-made speech-to-text → LLM → text-to-speech pipeline. A one-to-one call needs neither. Gemini and OpenAI both sell low-latency voice models that listen and speak directly ("realtime" APIs), and the browser can connect to them with the same key the learner already enters in Ascent.

- turning the whiteboard into text the interviewer can read

What we don't build: speech models or audio servers. Building those is where quality would suffer.

This also fits your earlier rule that keys stay in the browser. Agora's custom-model mode would have needed learners' AI keys on our server. For paid plans later, our server would hand out short-lived tokens, so subscribers never need a key at all.

Downsides of the realtime default:
- Cost: realtime audio is the most expensive engine per minute. The setup screen shows an estimate before each round, and a monthly budget can switch to a cheaper engine automatically.
- Gemini session limits: a Gemini Live connection lasts about 10 minutes, and audio-only sessions are reportedly capped near 15. A 45-minute round needs reconnect-and-resume handling, which is designed in.
- Claude: I found no Anthropic realtime voice API, so Claude users get Ascent Voice.

One correction to last session: Chrome's built-in speech recognition sends audio to Google unless on-device recognition is available. Browser voice is not automatically private.
